Two sources, zero overlap

I scored 438 startups against an AI-infrastructure thesis using only public SEC filings and the Y Combinator directory — no PitchBook, no Crunchbase. Changing one sentence in the thesis moved healthcare from 0% to 43% of the top 30. The two sources still never named the same company.

The problem

Most deal-sourcing tools start with a paid database. I wanted to know whether a first screen could be built from data anyone can pull: SEC Form D filings — the public record of companies raising private capital — and the Y Combinator directory, a free list of every company that accelerator has funded.

The thesis I scored against was specific: AI infrastructure and developer tools; Pre-Seed to Series A; US-based. The output is a 0–100 fit score, a comparable-company list, and a short memo for each name at the top.

What each source actually knows

The two feeds look complementary on paper. Form D gives a legal name, a location, and a dollar amount being raised. YC gives a website, a description, and a sector tag. Merge them and you should get a complete record.

On this snapshot they never met. In a 90-day SEC window, zero Form D filers matched a YC company on website or cleaned name. That is expected more than it is a bug: many YC companies raise on SAFEs that never trigger a public Form D, and YC is a thin slice of every startup currently fundraising. The lasting value of the merge step is the cleaning — stripping legal suffixes, fixing country labels from SEC state codes, collapsing repeat filings — not a flood of matches.

A paid database papers over this. Free data makes it visible: you are ranking two different kinds of company, and the score has to say so.

YC companies live on thesis match — a local sentence-transformer comparing the description to the thesis, worth up to 40 of the 100 points. Form D companies live on stage and geography, because that is what a filing actually contains. Traction is almost empty on both: GitHub, Hacker News, and public job boards were looked up for a capped set of ~40 companies with websites, and most startups simply do not have public engineering or hiring signal.

That is why the ceiling on this run is 58.7, not 90. The model is not failing to find winners. It is refusing to invent fields the sources do not provide.

Does the score actually follow the thesis?

Free public data has no answer key of which companies a partner would chase. So I did not fake investment returns. I checked two things the system can be wrong about.

Check Result
Share of healthcare in the top 30, AI-infra thesis 0%
Same list, thesis swapped to healthcare 43%
Top-5 comps in the same industry 77%
Same-industry rate by random chance 39%

Healthcare jumping from none of the top 30 to 43% of it is the load-bearing result. The ranking is not a frozen popularity list. Change the sentence and the order moves.

Comparables are about twice as coherent as chance: a company's five nearest neighbors share its industry 77% of the time against a 39% base rate. That is retrieval quality, not a claim that similar companies are good investments.

What this does not prove: that the top of the list is worth a meeting. Precision against real partner decisions would need those decisions. Until then the honest evaluation is behavioral — does the score respond, and are the comps on-topic — not returns.

The technical layer

For readers who want the mechanics
The 0–100 score

Six features, each 0 to 1, times an editable weight:

score = 100 × (0.40·semantic + 0.20·stage + 0.10·geo + 0.15·traction + 0.10·investor + 0.05·hygiene)

Semantic similarity is cosine distance between a local MiniLM embedding of the company description and the thesis text. Stage is inferred from the Form D offering amount against rough US venture buckets, because filings do not say "Seed." Traction combines GitHub stars/recency, Hacker News mentions, and open jobs, matched on website domain rather than name. Investor signal is repeat filings plus a real offering amount. Hygiene is whether website, description, year, and industry are present.

Unknown features are set to 0.5, not 0. Zero would systematically punish whichever source did not provide the field. Neutral keeps the ranking about the fields that exist, and the point breakdown still shows what is missing.

Why the two sources do not merge

Rows are treated as the same company if they share a website domain, a cleaned name (legal suffixes stripped), or a near-identical name in the same US state. On this run the YC–Form D overlap was zero. Repeat Form D filings for the same issuer do collapse.

Widening the SEC date window, or looking up websites for Form D issuers, would raise the match rate. A 90-day slice is too short to expect YC and current fundraising to coincide, and YC companies often raise in ways that never appear as Form D.

What the enrichment actually covered

GitHub, Hacker News, and public job boards are rate-limited, so enrichment is capped (about 40 website-having companies per run). Of 438 names, 38 had a GitHub lookup and 3 had any stars; 12 had Hacker News points; 3 had open jobs on Greenhouse or Lever.

Empty traction is often the truth, not a failed scrape. Very early and non-software companies do not have public repos. The notebook reports coverage rather than filling gaps. That sparsity is why traction averages 0.33 points for YC and 0 for Form D — and why a company like InsForge can jump the list with 12,521 stars when almost nobody else has the field at all.

What I'd do differently

Look up websites for Form D issuers. Without a domain, they cannot be enriched or matched to YC, so they remain a parallel list scored on stage and geo.

Widen the SEC window and raise the enrichment cap once a GitHub token is in the environment. Traction is the feature most likely to change the ranking if coverage improved.

Evaluate against real decisions when they exist. Thesis-swap and comp-coherence are the right tests for a system with no labels. They are not a substitute for "did a partner take the meeting."

What this case taught me

A screen can be built without a paid database. A complete record cannot. SEC filings know money and location. YC knows what the company says it does. Treating those as one universe hides the join. Leaving them side by side, with a score that shows which fields fired, is more honest.

Neutral is a modeling choice, not a missing value bug. Scoring absent fields as zero would have made every Form D company look worse than it is, and every YC company look like it had no round. The 0.5 prior is the thing that lets two incomplete sources share a ranking.

If you cannot score returns, score the behavior of the score. A healthcare thesis pulling healthcare into 43% of the top 30 is evidence the system listens. It is not evidence the system picks winners. Those are different claims, and only one of them is available on free public data.
