Response isn't revenue

Asked which email would make a customer most likely to buy, my model sent the endurance-sports message to 70% of the list. Asked which email would make the company the most money, it sent that same message to 41%. Same customers, same model — one word changed in the question.

The setup

A European sporting-goods retailer emails promotions to a very large customer base. Each department — endurance, strength, water sports, team sports, backcountry, racquet — competes for slots in the email calendar, and the existing policy allocated those slots at random.

The proposal was to allocate by prediction instead: for each customer, work out which department's message is worth the most, and send that one. Seven options per customer, counting the choice to send nothing at all.

Profit per email is straightforward, given that cost of goods runs at 60%:

expected profit = P(purchase) × predicted order size × 0.40

Which means there are two ways to pick a winner, and they are not the same.

The same model, two questions

Ranking each customer's seven options by probability of purchase gives one allocation. Ranking them by expected profit gives another. Toggle between them.

The reshuffle is dramatic. Endurance falls from 69.9% to 40.8%. Strength very nearly disappears — 20.3% down to 4.6%. And two departments that were almost never the response winner become major allocations: backcountry climbs from 0.8% to 16.7%, team sports from 0.9% to 15.8%.

The mechanism is order size. Endurance and strength messages produce lots of small purchases — running socks, protein powder. Backcountry and team messages produce fewer, larger ones — a tent, a full kit for a season. Multiply a smaller probability by a much bigger basket and the ranking inverts.

Note also what happens to the no-message option. Under the response objective it wins for zero customers, because sending something always beats sending nothing if all you care about is purchase probability. Under the profit objective it wins for 1.9% — the customers whose likely basket is too small to justify the contact. Only the profit framing can ever tell you to leave someone alone.

Personalization returns €0.7045 a customer. The best single message — endurance for everyone — returns €0.6269. The status quo, random allocation, returns €0.5600.

That last comparison is the uncomfortable one. Random allocation performs worse than four of the six individual messages. The company was not merely failing to personalize; it was actively diluting a good message with worse ones. Picking one decent message and sending it to everybody would have captured €334,767 of the €722,562 available — roughly 46% of the benefit, with no model at all.

Half the value of "personalization" here was really the value of not randomizing. That distinction matters when someone is deciding whether to fund a modelling project, and it is easy to leave out of the pitch.

The technical layer

For readers who want the mechanics
Building a seven-way choice from two models

Purchase probability comes from a logistic regression on demographics, per-department purchase history, and message type — with message × customer interactions, which is what allows different customers to respond differently to the same message. Without those interactions every customer would rank the seven messages identically and personalization would be impossible by construction.

Order size comes from a separate linear regression on the same predictors. Each customer is then scored seven times, once per message, and the two predictions are multiplied and margined.

I also fitted neural network, random forest and gradient boosting versions. Personalized profit came out around €0.69 for the neural network and €0.64 for random forest, against €0.7045 for the logistic model — the same ordering of policies in every case.

The order-size model needs a closer look

Expected profit multiplies P(purchase) by predicted order size, and that is only correct if order size means order size given that they purchase.

Order size is observed only for buyers. If the regression is fitted across all customers, with non-buyers entered as zero, then it is already predicting an unconditional average — most of which is zeros — and multiplying it by the purchase probability applies the same discount twice. The result would be too low overall, and biased against messages with lower response rates.

The correct construction fits order size on buyers only. I would want to confirm which was done before defending the euro figures, and it is the first thing I would check on returning to this analysis. The direction of the reshuffle — bigger baskets outranking likelier ones — holds either way, since it comes from relative order sizes rather than their level.

Why the personalized number is optimistic

Personalized profit is computed by taking, for each customer, the maximum of seven noisy predictions. Uniform policies involve no maximum at all.

Taking a maximum over noisy estimates is systematically biased upward — whichever option happens to have positive error is the one selected. With seven options and weak signal, that selection bias is not negligible, and it inflates personalization's advantage specifically, because none of the comparison policies are subject to it.

The honest measurement is to assign each test customer their model-recommended message and score the policy against realized outcomes. Since the underlying experiment randomized messages, the data to do that exists: take the customers who happened to receive the message the model would have chosen, and compare their actual profit against the full test average. That converts the estimate from a model-scored projection into something closer to a field result.

What I'd do differently

Score the policy on realized outcomes, as above. It is the single change that would move this from a promising projection to a defensible business case.

Verify the conditional order-size specification, and if it was fitted on all customers, refit on buyers and re-run the allocation.

Report the no-model baseline prominently. "Stop randomizing" captures 46% of the gain and requires no infrastructure. A recommendation that leads with personalization and buries that comparison is not giving decision-makers what they need.

Respect the frequency constraint. The case restricts how often a customer can be emailed and allocates department slots monthly. My analysis optimizes a single send in isolation; a real policy has to allocate a limited number of contacts across a month without exhausting anyone, which is a scheduling problem sitting on top of this one and genuinely harder.

What this case taught me

The objective function is the model. Everything else here was held constant — same data, same fitted coefficients, same customers — and switching from probability to profit reallocated two-thirds of the list. A model doesn't answer "what should we do." It answers exactly the question you encoded, and the encoding is a business decision wearing technical clothes.

Likelihood and value are different quantities, and only one of them pays. The message most likely to trigger a purchase is not the message worth the most, whenever purchase sizes differ. Optimizing conversion rate is optimizing a proxy — usually a decent one, sometimes badly wrong.

Always price the boring alternative. Personalization was worth €722,562 against the status quo. Doing nothing clever — just picking one good message instead of drawing at random — was worth €334,767 of that. The sophisticated answer is more defensible when you have measured how much of its advantage the simple answer already delivers.
